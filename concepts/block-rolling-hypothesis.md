# Block Rolling Hypothesis

An alternative to dragging blocks on sledges: attaching wooden cradles or reel-like structures around blocks to convert them from rectangular prisms into cylinders, allowing them to be rolled rather than dragged.

## The Core Idea

Instead of dragging a 2.5-tonne block on a sledge (coefficient of friction ~0.15-0.25 even with lubrication), the block is fitted with four wooden cradles — one per long edge — that transform it into a cylinder. Rolling resistance is dramatically lower than sliding friction.

## Physics Comparison

| Method | Coefficient | Force for 2.5t block | Workers needed (10% ramp) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dragging on sledge (dry) | μ ~0.3-0.7 | ~7,350-17,150 N | ~45-100+ |
| Dragging on sledge (water-lubricated) | μ ~0.15-0.25 | ~3,675-6,125 N | ~45 (Stocks) |
| Rolling with cradles (hard surface) | Crr ~0.001-0.01 | ~245-2,450 N | ~5-15 |

Rolling resistance (Crr ~0.001-0.01 on hard surfaces) is fundamentally lower than sliding friction (μ ~0.3-0.7) because it replaces sliding friction losses with hysteresis losses in the rolling surface. The force reduction could be 10-100x, dramatically easing the [[construction-methods]] throughput problem.

## Historical Evidence — or Lack Thereof

### What the Evidence Shows (Dragging, Not Rolling)
- **Djehutihotep tomb painting** (12th Dynasty): 172 men pulling a 60-tonne alabaster statue on a sledge, with a man pouring liquid ahead of the runners. This depicts dragging, not rolling.
- **[[diary-of-merer]]** (4,500 years old, discovered 2013): Documents boat transport of blocks from Tura to Giza. No mention of rolling.
- **Nova experiment** (1992, [[lehner]] & Hopkins): Confirmed rolling was possible for blocks under 1 ton with 4-5 men using levers — but this was levering/rolling for positioning, not transport.

### What Is Missing
- **No depictions of rolling methods** exist in any known Egyptian tomb painting, relief, or text.
- **No cradles or reel-like artifacts** have been found at Giza or any other pyramid site.
- The cradle-like objects from New Kingdom temples (cited by Parry) are 1,000+ years later than the Great Pyramid and their function is debated.

## Dick Parry's Theory

Dick Parry proposed a rolling method based on cradle-like objects excavated at New Kingdom temples. Four of those objects could be fitted around a block so it could be rolled easily. John Bush (1977) described a similar method. Vitruvius in *De architectura* described a similar method for moving irregular weights.

The Obayashi Corporation experimented with concrete blocks (0.8 m square × 1.6 m long, 2.5 tonnes) and found 18 men could drag the block over a 1-in-4 incline ramp at 18 m/min — though this experiment tested dragging, not rolling.

## How This Would Change the Construction Model

If rolling was used for transport and dragging/levering only for final positioning:
- The workforce needed for block transport could be reduced by 50-80%.
- Multiple parallel "rolling tracks" could operate with fewer workers each.
- The throughput problem (1 block per 2-3 minutes, see [[construction-methods]]) becomes more tractable.
- The wood required for cradles (Lebanese cedar) was already being imported for sledges and other purposes.

## Limitations

1. **Corners**: Rolling works well in straight lines but is extremely difficult around corners or on spiral ramps where turning is needed. The Great Pyramid's geometry (shrinking courses at higher levels) would require frequent direction changes.
2. **Placement precision**: Rolling is good for transport but blocks must be precisely positioned (sub-mm tolerances for casing stones, see [[construction-methods]]). Rolling would need to be combined with a levering system for final placement.
3. **Size limitations**: The Nova experiment showed rolling works for blocks under 1 ton with 4-5 men. The 80-tonne granite beams from [[internal-structure]] cannot be rolled.
4. **Block shape**: Great Pyramid core blocks are roughly dressed, non-uniform. Rolling works best for regular shapes. Casing stones (regular, finely dressed) would be more suitable for rolling.
5. **Wood scarcity**: Egypt had little wood. Cradles would require imported Lebanese cedar. However, wood was already imported for sledges.
6. **No archaeological evidence**: No cradles, no wear patterns on blocks consistent with rolling, no textual or iconographic evidence.
7. **Ramp slope complications**: Rolling on a 10% ramp is feasible, but on steeper gradients the block could roll backward uncontrollably — a safety concern that dragging doesn't have.

## Assessment

The block rolling hypothesis is physically sound but archaeologically unsupported. It could have been used as a supplement to dragging for certain block types (regular casing stones on straight ramp sections), but there is no evidence it was. The absence of evidence is not proof of absence — wooden cradles would have rotted away in 4,600 years — but the complete lack of iconographic or textual evidence in a culture that documented its construction processes (per the [[diary-of-merer]]) is significant.

The most plausible role for rolling: a hybrid approach where blocks were rolled on flat transport causeways (from quarry to pyramid base) and then dragged/levered up ramps for placement. This would optimize the most labor-intensive phase (long-distance transport) while using proven methods for the precision phase (placement).
